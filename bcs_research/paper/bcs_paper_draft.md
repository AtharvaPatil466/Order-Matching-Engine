## Abstract

Budish, Cramton, and Shim show that the continuous limit order book's latency arms race imposes a tax; which participants bear it is a separate question. We study it on a nanosecond discrete-event model in which a maker's cancel and snipers' orders race to a C++ matching engine whose design is model-checked in TLA+, with a jump-diffusion fundamental, order flow and fees taken from the BTCUSDT-perpetual tape, and with eleven predictions — the first that the model recovers BCS's own equilibrium — fixed in a pre-registration before any confirmatory run. ## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The arms-race argument

Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015, hereafter BCS) argue that the continuous limit order book's serial processing creates a mechanical arbitrage on every public price move: whoever reacts first picks off liquidity providers' stale quotes before they can be revised. The resulting race over speed rather than price dissipates resources and taxes liquidity provision without improving price discovery, and BCS propose frequent batch auctions — discrete-time, uniform-price clearing — to turn competition on speed back into competition on price.

### 1.2 The question and the design

This paper asks which participants bear the latency tax. The BCS argument establishes that the tax exists; it does not settle who pays it — makers or the investors who trade with them, and, among competing makers, which ones. The answer depends on how the race is run: on whether a maker's cancel can beat a snipe, on how makers quote, and on the fees each side pays.

An earlier version of this paper answered on a 100 ms tick model. It reported that a single maker gains from the race at the snipe size measured on the tape, and that which of several makers pays is set by how their latencies are spaced. That model's clock could not represent the race between a cancel and a snipe, and two of its results came from when it sampled the book (§3.8). We therefore rebuilt the model on a nanosecond event clock over the same verified engine (§3), recalibrated it to what the tape can resolve (§3.9), and wrote down eleven predictions before running it (§3.11). Several predict that the earlier headlines will not survive. Each is reported as a hit, a miss or inconclusive (§4).

## 2. Related Literature

**The arms race and its measurement.** Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015) treat latency competition as a consequence of the continuous book's serial processing rather than as conduct to be regulated, and propose frequent batch auctions as the remedy. Aquilina, Budish, and O'Neill (2022) measure the race directly from message-level data that record failed attempts: races occur about once a minute per FTSE 100 symbol, are decided by microseconds, and sum to a latency-arbitrage tax of about 0.4 basis points of volume. Neither paper asks which participants bear the tax. The closest evidence on that question is Shkilko and Sokolov (2020): when weather disrupts the microwave links that give the fastest traders their edge, adverse selection falls and trading costs decline, in a market with many competing liquidity suppliers. Menkveld (2016) surveys HFT's double role as liquidity supplier and source of adverse selection and notes that the net welfare sign for any participant class is unsettled. Menkveld (2013) and Brogaard, Hendershott, and Riordan (2014) place fast traders on the maker side of the book as well as the sniping side, which is why incidence within the liquidity-providing class is a separate question from incidence on the class.

**Speed and exchange design.** Menkveld and Zoican (2017) show that a faster exchange can worsen liquidity, because it speeds up snipers and makers alike and raises the frequency of speed duels. Baldauf and Mollner (2020) compare batch auctions with asymmetric speed bumps, and Du and Zhu (2017) derive the optimal batching frequency as a trade-off between information aggregation and delayed incorporation. Hoffmann (2014) shows that fast traders concentrate pick-off risk on slow limit-order submitters, who respond by quoting less aggressively, and Biais, Foucault, and Moinas (2015) show that equilibrium investment in speed exceeds the social optimum because the fast impose adverse selection on the slow. These models derive who is fast and what speed costs in equilibrium. We impose the latencies, let the makers' quoting range from imposed rules to best responses over a strategy space (P8), and ask how incidence varies with market structure.

**Adverse selection and the spread.** Copeland and Galai (1983) model the spread as a trade-off between revenue from uninformed traders and losses to informed ones, and Glosten and Milgrom (1985) make the informed-trading component explicit. Foucault, Kozhan, and Tham (2017) show empirically that arbitrages triggered by news, rather than by transient imbalance, are the ones that widen spreads. Because widening is at once a defence against the informed leg and a tax on the uninformed one, the maker's two legs are tested separately (P7, §4.4) rather than only their difference.

**Agent-based models.** The agent-based computational finance tradition (Farmer and Foley, 2009; LeBaron, 2006) argues that simulations of heterogeneous agents can produce out-of-equilibrium phenomena that representative-agent models cannot; its known weaknesses are ad hoc behavioural assumptions, weak calibration, and simulation machinery that is hard to verify. Wah and Wellman (2013) built an agent-based model of latency arbitrage across fragmented markets and found that the arbitrageur profits at other traders' expense while degrading allocative efficiency, the same qualitative transfer we decompose. The method is therefore not new. What this paper adds is the substrate and the question: the BCS mechanism runs on a matching engine whose design is model-checked and has been tested differentially against a re-implementation (§§3.1, 5), with accounting whose identities hold to rounding (§3.6), and the question asked of it, under pre-registered predictions, is who bears the tax rather than whether it exists.

## 3. Model and Experimental Design

One C++ matching engine makes every trade. A Python layer above it supplies the clock, the agents, the fundamental and the accounting, and a pybind11 bridge between the two marks where the engine's formal guarantees end. This section describes the nanosecond event model on which every result in §4 is computed; §3.8 says why the 100 ms tick model of the paper's earlier version was replaced.

### 3.1 The engine, the bridge, and what the verification covers

All trades, in continuous matching and in batch uncrosses alike, are produced by one C++20 matching engine, reached from Python through a pybind11 bridge that is the agents' only channel to it. The engine runs synchronously, so the fills an order produces are drained immediately after it is submitted, and it has no notion of latency: the race lives entirely in the scheduler above it (§3.2). Its circuit breaker is disabled, so that the simulation rather than an exchange halt governs the dynamics. The bridge exposes order entry, cancels, top of book, depth snapshots, the session states and the call-auction `uncross`, and it reports every order the book refuses after admission, so that a refusal reaches the scheduler instead of leaving an agent believing a quote rests (Supplement S3.3).

The engine's matching design is model-checked in TLA+. At its deepest exhaustive configuration the continuous-matching specification explores 171,187,419 distinct reachable states with zero invariant violations, checking FIFO execution and conservation; the auction specification checks that every uncross clears at a single price, the path the batch arm runs through. The verification has two limits. TLC's guarantee is exhaustive only within small bounds — a handful of orders, two prices, short horizons — so it certifies the matching *design* at bounded scope rather than proving unbounded correctness. And the object verified is the specification, not the C++ source: no refinement proof links the two, and the correspondence rests on construction and conformance testing. Nothing above the bridge is verified. The scheduler, agents and accounting are checked by unit tests and, as a whole, by requiring the model to recover BCS's closed-form equilibrium before it is used for anything else (P1, §4.1). Supplement S3 lists the specifications, invariants and configurations; §5 reports a differential test of the engine's clearing rule.

### 3.2 The scheduler and its latency semantics

The scheduler is a discrete-event loop on an integer-nanosecond clock (`simulation/event_scheduler.py`, implementing the contract in `simulation/contracts.py`). Every event — an agent observing a public update, an order or cancel reaching the engine, a fill reported back, a self-scheduled wakeup — carries its own timestamp. Each agent has an observation latency and an order latency, and the scheduler applies them as follows.

- **Observation.** An agent observes a public update at the update's time plus its observation latency, and its own fills at the execution time plus the same latency.
- **Orders and jitter.** Orders and cancels returned from a callback at time $t$ reach the engine at $t$ plus the agent's order latency plus a race jitter drawn uniformly from $\{0,\dots,\varepsilon\}$ ns. Each agent draws its jitter from its own seeded stream, so adding or removing an agent — the treatment in a matched pair of runs — leaves every other agent's draws unchanged.
- **Bursts and per-agent FIFO.** One callback's messages travel as one burst: they arrive together and apply in the order returned, so a maker's requote cannot overtake, and trade against, its own stale quote. Successive bursts from one agent arrive in send order however wide its jitter; a burst that would overtake or tie an earlier one is moved to one nanosecond after it.
- **Ties.** Events due at the same nanosecond run in a seeded random permutation, never in registration order. Events created at an instant with zero delay form a fresh batch at that instant, run after everything already due, so a zero-latency agent cannot act on news before every other agent has had the chance to observe it.
- **Rejects.** When the engine refuses a message — an order at admission or after it, or a cancel of an order already gone — the sender learns of it its observation latency after the refusal. A post-only quote that would cross never rests, and an agent that could not learn this would keep it in the book it believes it has.
- **Book reads.** Agents read the engine's current top of book. Book-data latency is not modelled; the race is driven by the latency of the public signal.

For every jump the scheduler logs the engine arrivals that agents' reactions to that jump produced, in engine order, each with its outcome (filled, missed, cancelled, too late). Whether the maker's cancel beat the first snipe is a lookup in that log, and race-win rates are counts over it. Top-of-book snapshots are taken every 100 ms at the operating point, the tape's book cadence.

### 3.3 The cancel-versus-snipe race

A jump makes the maker's resting quotes stale. The maker, on observing it, sends one burst that cancels them and posts new quotes; each sniper, on observing it, sends one immediate-or-cancel (IOC) order at the stale price if the quote is stale by more than its cost. The engine applies the messages in arrival order. If the maker's cancel arrives first, every snipe finds nothing at that price and dies, and because a sniper never sends a market order a losing snipe cannot lift the maker's new quote. If a snipe arrives first, it fills against the stale quote. There is no race-specific code: the outcome is set by latencies and jitter draws alone.

With every racing message drawing an independent $U[0,\varepsilon]$ jitter on top of its fixed latency, a maker whose total latency exceeds the snipers' by $\ell$ beats all $k$ snipers with probability
$$
P_{\text{win}}(\ell,\varepsilon,k) = \frac{1}{k+1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon-\ell}{\varepsilon}\right)^{k+1} \quad (0\le \ell\le\varepsilon), \qquad 0 \quad (\ell>\varepsilon),
$$
derived in `analysis/bcs_theory.py`. At $\ell = 0$ it is BCS's $1/N$ with $N = k+1$ racing firms. P2 tests the formula directly and P7 tests the loss it implies (§4.3, §4.4). In the tick model a maker and a sniper reacting to the same news acted in the same 100 ms tick, and the race between them was a jitter draw inside it (§3.8).

### 3.4 The fundamental

The fundamental $V(t)$ is a jump-diffusion published as discrete updates (`agents/fundamental_paths.py`). A Brownian diffusion moves on a fixed grid, every 10 ms at the operating point, and compound-Poisson jumps arrive at rate $\lambda_j$ anywhere in integer-nanosecond time with signed Laplace$(0,b)$ sizes, so the absolute jump $J$ is exponential with mean $b$. Every change in $V$ is a public update, and jump updates are flagged for the race log. The path is generated whole and seed-deterministically before a run, the diffusion drawn first so that runs differing only in jump settings share it. Parameters enter in physical units — diffusive volatility in bp/$\sqrt{\text{s}}$ with jumps excluded, jump rate per second, $b$ in bp — and are converted once as fractions of the starting value $v_0$. Steps are arithmetic in price, which is accurate while moves stay small relative to $v_0$, as they do over a 30-minute run. Agents trade only on the updates they observe; the true $V$ is read by the accounting, by the maker's adverse-fill test and by the slow informed investor (§3.5).

Laplace jumps are the default because BCS's equilibrium has a closed form for them. The per-jump sniping prize at half-spread $h$ is $b\,e^{-h/b}$, and BCS's equation (3), $\lambda_i h = \lambda_j\,b\,e^{-h/b}$ with $\lambda_i$ the investor arrival rate, gives the equilibrium half-spread $h^* = b\,W(\lambda_j/\lambda_i)$, $W$ the Lambert function (derived in `analysis/bcs_theory.py`). A correlated-signal variant is implemented for news that arrives through a lead instrument: the lead path $L$ publishes, the traded asset is worth $V = v_0 + \beta(L - v_0) + I(t)$ with $I$ an idiosyncratic Gaussian walk, and updates carry $E[V\mid L]$ while $V$ itself is never published. It is unit-tested, and no pre-registered experiment uses it.

### 3.5 Agents

Every agent implements one interface — callbacks on start, on an observed update, on a wakeup and on a fill, each returning orders, cancels and wakeups — and nothing else connects it to the scheduler.

**Makers.** One class executes any maker strategy given as a `MakerPolicy` (`agents/strategic_maker.py`), a small frozen value that a best-response search can perturb, compare and cache (P8). Its dimensions are: whether to quote at all, the no-quote option that gives entry and exit a margin; the base half-spread and size; a ladder of levels behind the touch; Avellaneda–Stoikov (2008) quoting with risk aversion $\gamma$ over a rolling horizon, with the order-arrival decay tied to the base half-spread so that $\gamma = 0$ is fixed-spread quoting; linear inventory skew and an inventory limit; adverse-selection widening; quote fading after a jump or an adverse fill; a requote threshold; requoting at once after a fill; and post-only quotes, which the engine refuses rather than lets cross. The default policy is BCS's liquidity provider: a fixed half-spread around the last update observed, cancelled and requoted in one burst on every update. The widening dimension carries the earlier version's maker onto the event clock: a fill adverse against the true $V$ at execution multiplies the half-spread by one plus a sensitivity, up to a cap; the multiple decays toward base on every observed update; and resting size can thin by the same factor. The true $V$ enters only that adverse-fill test.

**Snipers.** A sniper reacts only to news (`agents/event_sniper.py`). On observing an update it compares the new value with the current top of book and, if a resting quote is stale by more than its threshold, sends one IOC at that stale price for its snipe size; otherwise it does nothing. Its threshold is its marginal cost, the taker fee of the run's schedule.

**Investors.** Uninformed flow is a Poisson population of reservation-value investors (`agents/event_noise.py`). Arrival $i$ values the asset at the last update it observed plus a private shock $\eta_i \sim N(0,s_\eta^2)$; it buys at the ask if that value is at least the ask, sells at the bid if it is at most the bid, and otherwise leaves. Participation therefore falls as the spread widens without an elasticity parameter: with the signal at the mid and half-spread $h$, an arrival crosses with probability $2\Phi(-h/s_\eta)$, and an infinite $s_\eta$ recovers the tick model's fixed-probability crossers. Sizes are lognormal.

**The slow informed investor.** A long-horizon investor reads the true $V$ with a lag at fixed decision times and, when its forecast differs from the mid, works a parent order as a series of IOC children at the touch. It is the one agent that reads $V$ directly, because learning $V$ late is what makes it informed. It is not part of the operating point; P6 adds it, reading $V$ one second late, to test BCS's claim that information slower than the quote-setter's is worthless in a continuous book (§4.4).

### 3.6 Accounting: fees, rebates, the cost of speed, and two identities

Welfare is one fold over the engine's trade list (`metrics/accounting.py`), so it cannot drift from what executed. Every fill pays the maker or the taker fee in basis points of notional; the maker is the non-aggressor, and a negative maker fee is a rebate. Fees are transfers to the exchange, which gives two identities that hold to rounding in every run: gross trading P&L sums to zero across participants at any common mark, and net P&L plus exchange revenue sums to zero. Speed is a third leg. A participant can be charged for its latency, and that money leaves the market for fibre and towers; it is the resource cost BCS call waste, so it is reported beside the identities and never inside them (P4). Open inventory is marked twice, at the last two-sided mid and at the true $V$ at the end of the run, and rents are read at $V$. Where a prediction concerns profit per event, fills are scored instead as edge against $V$ at execution, BCS's payoff, which has the same mean without the terminal mark's inventory noise. The operating point charges makers nothing and takers 1.7 bp, Binance USDⓈ-M's lowest public taker tier (VIP 9); P5 adds a zero schedule, a taker-heavy one at the regular tier's 2 bp maker and 5 bp taker, and a stylised maker-rebate one at −1 and 3 bp.

### 3.7 Frequent batch auctions on the engine's uncross

The batch arm (`simulation/event_batch.py`) keeps the scheduler's clock, latencies, jitter, FIFO and tie rule, and replaces matching on arrival with BCS's frequent batch auction (BCS §VII). Interval $k$ is the half-open window $[k\tau, (k+1)\tau)$, and its uncross runs at $(k+1)\tau$ before any arrival at that nanosecond, so an arrival on a boundary belongs to the next batch. Cancels take effect on arrival, so a cancel received during an interval is honoured before that interval's uncross. New orders are held until the boundary, handed to the engine in arrival order — or, in a rationing arm, in a seeded shuffle — and cleared by the engine's own `uncross`; orders resting from earlier batches keep priority. An IOC means "this auction only": it enters the uncross as a limit and its remainder is cancelled immediately after, so it never rests. The book is sealed: agents and snapshots see it as it stood after the last uncross. The engine stamps every uncross trade with a buy aggressor, so for fees the scheduler makes the later arrival the taker and an order resting from an earlier batch the maker.

The engine's clearing rule is not BCS's. It ranks candidate prices by executable volume, then imbalance, then distance to a reference price, then market pressure. The reference is the session's first limit order, and `uncross` never updates it, so volume-and-imbalance ties resolve toward where the session opened rather than toward the last print (verified in `tests/test_event_batch.py`). With one stale ask at $A$ and two or more snipers bidding $V' > A$, every price in $[A, V']$ ties on volume and imbalance, and the engine clears at the stale price $A$ whenever its reference lies nearer $A$; BCS's Bertrand argument (Prop. 6(iii)) clears at $V'$. A second price rule, "pressure", removes the reference step: the engine still decides who trades and how much, and the scheduler moves the price to the reference-free cascade's only when that price clears the same volume and lies inside every filled order's limit, so the fills are exactly those at the new price; otherwise the engine's price stands and the refusal is counted. P9 runs both rules and reads its verdicts on the engine as it is (§4.5). The theory supplies the exposure: a liquidity provider slower than the snipers by $\delta$ is exposed on a share $\delta/\tau$ of jumps (Prop. 6(i)); a fast one only through jitter, on a share $\varepsilon/(2\tau)$ (footnote 34), which we refine to $(\varepsilon/\tau)(N-1)/(2(N+1))$ by requiring a sniper to make the batch the provider missed.

### 3.8 The earlier tick model

The paper's earlier version ran on a tick model: every agent acted on a 100 ms grid, the fundamental was a Gaussian random walk stepped each tick, the maker observed it three ticks late, snipers reacted within the tick, and noise traders crossed with a fixed probability per tick. It was replaced because its clock could not represent the race. A maker and a sniper reacting to the same news acted in the same tick, and the race between them was a jitter draw inside it; a random walk has no jumps, which is where BCS's rents come from (P3); the growth of liquidity gaps with the number of snipers, and the decline of rent with it, came from where the end-of-tick snapshot fell relative to the snipes; and its batch arm entered snipers' IOCs as limits at the stale price, which let them outbid the maker for noise flow inside a batch. Its results are not carried over. The claims the pre-registration re-tests appear in §4 as predictions; Supplement S5 describes the model, the retired results and the reason for each.

### 3.9 Calibration to the BTCUSDT-perpetual tape

**Data, and what it can resolve.** The model's primitives are fitted to Binance BTCUSDT-perpetual order flow recorded by a companion collector: 20-level book snapshots at about 10 per second and the aggregated-trade stream. The sample is 27 days (2026-06-24 to 2026-07-21, less 2026-07-19): 37.0 million trade records over 2.27 million seconds of coverage, and 4.16 million book snapshots. Every moment is computed within hole-free hourly files and pooled, so no return or bucket straddles a collector gap (Supplement S2.1). The paper's earlier version said both streams carry nanosecond timestamps. They do not. A trade's exchange timestamp is Binance's millisecond stamp stored in nanosecond units, and none of the 37 million is finer than a millisecond; the collector's receive stamps are polling times and are not used. The aggregate-trade id has no gaps within a file, so the order of records inside a millisecond is exact. And on USDⓈ-M futures a record is not one taker order: Binance merges fills at the same price and taking side within 100 ms, across takers, and no two consecutive same-side, same-price records on the tape are less than 101 ms apart. Price changes are therefore stamped to the millisecond, but record counts are not taker-order counts, and several snipers hitting one stale level print as one record (`calibration/fine_grain.py`, `results/calibration/fine_grain_v2.json`).

**Order flow and book.** Records arrive at 16.28 per second; record sizes have p50, p90 and p99 of 0.003, 0.177 and 2.01 BTC; the median spread is one 0.10 USD tick (0.0158 bp); and the median top-of-book depth is 6.18 BTC. The operating point takes these as its investor crossing rate, its lognormal size law (median 3 lots of 0.001 BTC, the venue's quantity step, with $\sigma_{\ln} = 2.80$ fitted to the p50–p99 ratio), its price grid (one engine unit per 0.10 USD tick) and its aggregate quote of 6,180 lots.

**Jumps.** The fundamental's parameters come from a jump test on the trade-implied mid: the trade price less half a tick for a buy and plus half a tick for a sell, which removes bid-ask bounce when the spread is one tick (`calibration/jumps.py`). A Lee and Mykland (2008) threshold test runs per hole-free hour at a 1% level, with the local scale from bipower variation of one-minute returns; the diffusive volatility is the truncated realised variance, and the Laplace scale is the mean absolute jump. At the 10 ms update interval the fit is a diffusive $\sigma$ of 0.242 bp/$\sqrt{\text{s}}$, 0.200 jumps per second and a Laplace scale $b$ of 0.705 bp, with jumps carrying 78% of return variance (`results/calibration/jump_fit_v2.json`). The Laplace law matches the jumps' second moment to within 6% ($2b^2 = 0.995$ against a measured $E[J^2]$ of 1.055 bp²) but not their shape (Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance 0.27). The fit depends on the sampling interval: at 100 ms and 1 s it gives 0.037 and 0.004 jumps per second with $b$ of 1.49 and 3.97 bp, and the tape's total volatility rises from 0.52 to 0.65 bp/$\sqrt{\text{s}}$ between 10 ms and 1 s, which no Lévy path reproduces. The operating point uses the 10 ms fit, the cadence at which its fundamental publishes (Supplement Table S10).

**Races on the tape.** A public tape shows fills, not the failed orders that identify a race (Aquilina, Budish, and O'Neill, 2022), so we measure the footprint a race leaves and bound it (`calibration/race_proxy.py`). A trigger is a record that moved the trade-implied mid in its own direction; a race is two or more same-side records from the start of the trigger's millisecond to $W$ ms after it, for $W$ from 0 to 100. The upper bound counts every such burst, including one taker sweeping several levels. The lower bound counts only bursts spanning at least two millisecond stamps, which under 100 ms aggregation must involve at least two takers; it misses same-millisecond races and is zero at $W = 0$ by construction. The upper bound is 1.39 races per second at $W = 0$ and falls to 0.40 at 100 ms; the lower bound is 0.29 at 1 ms, peaks at 0.53 at 5 ms and is 0.35 at 100 ms (Supplement Table S11).

**What the races follow (exploratory).** The proxy triggers on any price move, and an exploratory analysis, not pre-registered and with its thresholds set before it ran, sorted the races by the size of the move behind them: from the implied mid before a race's first millisecond to the furthest implied mid its own records reach (`calibration/race_by_move_size.py`; `paper/race_gap_note.md`; `results/calibration/race_by_move_size_v2.json`; 95% intervals from a day-block bootstrap over the 27 days). Of the same-millisecond races, 99% follow moves smaller than the 1.7 bp taker fee, and 97% of the lower-bound races do. Restricted to moves that clear the fee plus a half-spread, the tape shows 0.0096 races a second [0.0070, 0.0126] at the upper bound and 0.0083 [0.0064, 0.0105] at the lower, and 79% of such moves are followed by a race within the same millisecond, 86% within 1 ms. The moves themselves arrive at 0.0087 a second [0.0065, 0.0112] on a 10 ms grid, 0.49 [0.36, 0.63] times the rate of the model as run; on 100 ms and 1 s grids the ratio is 0.79 [0.57, 1.06] and 1.67 [1.26, 2.14], because the tape's fee-sized moves include slower ones a jump-diffusion lacks (Supplement Tables S12–S13).

**Race size against depth.** The incidence predictions are indexed by a race's size relative to the top-of-book depth it met. At $W = 0$, against the hit side's level-one size in the last snapshot at most 200 ms old, the median is 0.052 (p90 2.7; 550,014 races). Against snapshots at most 20 ms old it is 0.18 (p90 9.3; 109,036 races), because a stale snapshot shows depth that has since been taken; 19% of races exceed the displayed depth (`results/calibration/race_proxy_v2.json`). The earlier version's single figure, a race-proxy median of 0.0283, triggered on 100 ms book snapshots and regrouped records under the spot market's aggregation rule — one record per taker order and price level — which futures records, merged across takers, do not follow. The pre-registration therefore evaluates every count over "tape-band" cells on the corrected ratios {0.052, 0.18} beside the paper's original {0.0049, 0.0097} (§3.11).

**Stylized facts.** Table 1 measures the tape, the tick model and the event model with one function (`analysis/stylized_facts.py`). The event-model column comes from the facts pilot — ten seeds of one hour at a pre-sign-off operating point that differs from §3.10's in its race — three snipers 90 µs faster than the one maker (5 + 5 µs against 50 + 50 µs), with no jitter — and in including the slow informed investor. The event model matches the tape's spread, mean trade rate, volatility and long-horizon return autocorrelation, where the tick model's spread was 76 times the tape's and its 1 s volatility twelve times. Its price impact is small and persistent, as the tape's is, but about a third of its size. It misses everything that depends on clustered or split order flow: trade counts are close to Poisson (Fano factor 0.79 against 125), trade signs carry no memory where the tape's lag-1 sign autocorrelation is 0.59, and almost no records share a millisecond.

The event model is therefore a mechanism model with tape-calibrated primitives, not a market replica. Its jump law, order-flow rate and sizes, price grid, depth and fees come from the tape and the venue. Its strategic structure — who is fast, by how much, and how makers quote — is designed and swept, because no public feed reveals it.

### 3.10 The operating point

The operating point was fixed in the pre-registration's sign-off table before any confirmatory run; `experiments/operating_point.py` holds it once, and a test pins each value. The fundamental is §3.9's 10 ms jump fit on a grid where one engine unit is the venue's 0.10 USD tick ($v_0 = 630{,}927$). Investors cross at the tape's 16.28 per second against the BCS half-spread, with §3.9's size law and a private-value scale of 0.52 bp, the path's total one-second volatility. Their arrival rate is fixed at the value that gives that crossing rate and is not re-solved per arm, so the investor stream is the same in every arm whatever the makers quote. Makers rest 6,180 lots in aggregate, split evenly across $M$ makers with the remainder to the first. The default maker quotes BCS's zero-profit half-spread at the fitted parameters, $b\,W(\lambda_j/\lambda_i) = 0.0086$ bp, about half a tick, so on the grid it quotes a one-tick spread. Maker latencies are one-way — observation and order latency both $L$, a reaction lag of $2L$ — and each prediction states its own; the default maker is as fast as the snipers. Snipers observe and send in 50 µs each, and every racing firm, makers included, draws $\varepsilon = 80$ µs of jitter per burst; investors do not race and draw none. Snipe size is a stated ratio of the aggregate quote. Fees are 0 for makers and 1.7 bp for takers, and a sniper's threshold is the taker fee. Runs last 30 simulated minutes, with $n = 100$ seeds per cell unless a prediction states otherwise. A seed fixes the path, the investors' arrivals and private values, and the scheduler's tie and jitter streams separately, so the arms of one experiment share their path and investors and every effect is a paired per-seed difference. Where a prediction concerns the earlier version's maker, that maker is re-implemented on the event clock with its calibrated sensitivity of 0.015, decay of 0.1 per 100 ms, cap of ten times base and size thinning, cancelling and requoting on every observed update, at 300 ms one-way latency unless the prediction states another.

### 3.11 Pre-registration and inference

Eleven predictions, P1–P11, were written before the event-model experiments they cover were run, and signed off on 2026-10-12 (`paper/PREREGISTRATION.md`). Each names a direction, a magnitude wherever BCS's theory or a derivation from it supplies one, a test, and the outcome that counts as a miss. Several state that a headline of the earlier version will not survive; the pre-registration's §3 lists them with the experiment that tests each. Every prediction ends as exactly one of HIT (its decision rule is met), MISS (its miss condition is met) or INCONCLUSIVE (neither). An inconclusive result is never counted as a hit, and if more than a third of the primaries are inconclusive the program is underpowered and §4 says so before anything else.

**Pilots and confirmatory runs.** Between the pre-registration's draft and its sign-off, pilot runs were made to build and size the experiments. They are kept apart (`results/pilot/`), each labelled as not a test, and this paper reports none of them as a result; the only pilot figures in it are the calibration diagnostics of Table 1 and one sniping rate in §6.2, both labelled. The confirmatory batch started after the sign-off was committed, at the operating point of §3.10, and every result in §4 comes from it.

**Amendments and clarifications.** Three amendments were approved at sign-off and logged with whether results had been seen. First, every count over "tape-band" cells — the earlier version's 18 cells, snipe-to-quote ratios {0.0049, 0.0097} by sniper counts {1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 13, 21} — is also evaluated on §3.9's corrected band {0.052, 0.18} and reported beside it; verdicts are read on the original band, as pre-registered. Second, a secondary prediction, P10b, was added because a pilot suggested it: the maker that most often holds time priority at the stale touch when a jump's first snipe arrives is the one that pays, computed in advance from the latency configuration by a predictor committed before the confirmatory run, which draws its configurations from a fresh generator seed. Third, P11's prediction about the noise-trader toll was recast as an equivalence test, because the toll is close to zero in absolute terms and a variance index on it is noise. Writing the confirmatory drivers then surfaced details the predictions left open, each fixed and logged before any confirmatory result existed (pre-registration §4.1). Those that bear on reading §4: the slow informed investor is outside the operating point; maker latencies are one-way; P9 reads the engine's reference price as it is, the session's first order, where P9 had assumed the last clearing price; P10b's verdict of record is read on the configurations where its predictor names a payer; and P11's verdict of record uses its written 512 base samples, with an extension to 2,048 reported beside it. Where a clarification departs from a literal reading, both readings are reported.

**Tests.** Intervals are 95% percentile bootstraps of the mean over seeds, 2,000 resamples with a fixed bootstrap seed; proportions use Wilson intervals. Each experiment has one primary hypothesis, and the eleven primaries form one family, Holm-adjusted (Holm, 1979) at $\alpha = 0.05$; secondary predictions use unadjusted 95% intervals and are reported as such. A magnitude predicted "within $\pm x$" is decided by a band rule: HIT if the 95% interval lies inside the band, MISS if it is disjoint from it, INCONCLUSIVE otherwise. The rule is not "the interval contains the prediction", which across a dozen cells would fail a correct model about half the time by chance. Predictions of no effect or of invariance are tested by two one-sided tests with the stated margin — HIT if the 90% interval lies inside it, MISS if the 95% interval is disjoint from it — never by a failure to reject.

## 5. Differential testing against the verified engine

Model checking certifies the matching design at bounded scope (§3.1). It says nothing about code that re-implements part of that design, and the harness contained such code. Testing it against the engine found a divergence that had moved published results.

**The test.** Until the bridge exposed the engine's call auction, the batch arm of the tick model's Experiment 4 (Supplement S5) cleared through a Python re-implementation of the clearing rule (`discover_uncross_price`, `simulation/batch_auction.py`), written from the engine's `OrderBook::discoverUncrossPrice` by reading the C++ source line by line. Once the bridge exposed `uncross` and the session-state controls (Supplement S3.3), both implementations could clear the same book. They agree on executable volume but not always on price. On a book with bids 102×50 and 101×30 against asks 99×40 and 100×60, eighty units clear at either 100 or 101 with equal imbalance; the Python rule clears at 100 and the engine at 101. Both apply the same cascade — maximise executable volume, minimise imbalance, minimise distance to a reference price, then market pressure — and on this book they part at the reference step. The engine anchors its reference on the first order's price before any trade (102 here) and picks 101; the Python rule has no reference until its first clearing, falls through to market pressure, and a sell surplus clears lower. A volume-based check cannot see the difference, and every metric that depends on execution price can. The case is pinned in `tests/test_batch_auction.py::test_python_matcher_divergence_pinned`, and the Python rule is kept only as a cross-check.

**The divergence moved published results, through that code path only.** Experiment 4's 50,000-tick grid has seven batch arms and one continuous arm, and the continuous arm never calls `uncross`. We re-ran the grid with batch clearing moved from the Python rule to the engine, holding seeds, fundamental paths, run length and every parameter fixed.

HFT rent crosses zero in three batch cells, from significantly positive to indistinguishable from zero with a negative point estimate, and the maker's delta changes significance in five. In the continuous control every maker, noise-trader and liquidity-gap value is bit-identical across the two runs, and HFT rent agrees to within $10^{-10}$ (578.1 [449, 709] both times), the floating-point residue of a change to how rent is summed that landed in the same commit. The control rules out seed variation, drift in the agent code and environment differences, and confines the change to the substituted code path. The constructed book shows that the two rules can disagree; the control shows that the disagreement reached published economic quantities and nothing else did.

**The tie-break is an economic assumption.** The engine's rule is correct relative to its own specification, not relative to any venue, and venues order these steps differently, so the port is also a comparison of two plausible tie-break rules. On the 3,000-tick grid the rule changes no batch cell's significance verdict for either the maker's delta or HFT rent. On the 50,000-tick grid it changes most of the batch arm: under the Python rule the maker loses at every interval from 5 to 100 ticks, recovering 40–68% of its continuous-clearing loss at intervals 5–25, while under the engine's rule it breaks even or gains, significantly at intervals 10 and 25. What holds under both rules is the comparison with continuous clearing: at every interval of five ticks or more, the maker does better batched than continuous. How much better, whether it ends up ahead, and whether batching removes the rent all depend on the tie-break. The tick batch arm had two further defects, unrelated to the tie-break: it gave a window's IOCs lower time priority than limits that arrived after them, and let same-price IOCs fill in submission order, so a latency race survived inside the batch (Supplement S5.2). The event model's batch (§3.7) replaces it.

The episode shows that a re-implementation checked against source code can still differ from the code it copies the first time both run on the same input. It does not show that the engine is correct; it shows that running against the engine caught an error that reading its source did not.

### 6.2 Limitations

**The jump law has the right rate near the fee but the wrong shape.** The tape's race proxy counts 0.29–1.39 races a second, against about 0.02 snipes a second in the facts pilot at the 1.7 bp fee, but the gap is the proxy's, not the model's: almost all of the proxy's races follow moves below the fee, and at moves that clear it the tape races 0.42–0.48 times as often as the pilot's snipers fill (exploratory, §3.9). What remains is the shape of the jump law. On a 10 ms grid the Laplace fit gives about twice the tape's rate of fee-clearing moves (0.0178 against 0.0087 a second) and about a sixth of its rate of moves of 5 bp or more (0.0002 against 0.0010), so matching the rate of races near the fee does not validate rent per snipe, which depends on how far jumps overshoot the fee. Over one second the tape clears the fee 1.67 times as often as the model, through slower moves that a jump-diffusion, whose volatility is the same at every horizon, lacks; whether such moves can be sniped depends on how long quotes stay stale. And the tape still shows neither participants nor failed orders: its upper bound counts one taker's sweep as a race, its lower bound misses same-millisecond races, and part of each measured move is the race's own impact (Supplement Tables S12–S13).

**The engine's limits.** The book is a fixed window of 200,001 price ticks centred on the first price it sees, with no re-centring; an order outside it is refused after admission, and the scheduler stops the run rather than continue with a maker missing from the book. At the operating point the window spans ±16% of $v_0$, far beyond a 30-minute path's range, but the replication's pure-jump path at BCS's scale would leave it, so P1 reverses a jump's sign whenever it would carry $V$ more than 90,000 ticks from its start, which keeps jump times and sizes and changes only the level path (`experiments/exp9_bcs_replication.py`). The uncross's reference price is the session's first order and is never updated, so with two or more snipers in a batch the engine clears a stale quote at the stale price where BCS's Bertrand outcome clears at the new value (§3.7). P9's verdicts read the engine as it is, with the reference-free "pressure" rule reported beside. And because the engine stamps every uncross trade with a buy aggressor, the batch arm's fee incidence — the later arrival pays the taker fee — is a modelling choice.

**Order flow is simpler than the tape's.** Investors arrive as a Poisson process, so trade counts do not cluster (Fano factor 0.79 against the tape's 125, Table 1). No investor splits a parent order at the operating point, so trade signs carry no memory; the slow informed investor's parent order is the model's only metaorder, and only P6 includes it. Makers quote one level by default against the tape's 20-level book, and only P7's ladder quotes more. Agents read the current book without delay, so only the signal's latency races. And the fundamental is a constant-intensity jump-diffusion fitted at one horizon, whose Laplace law matches the jumps' second moment but not their shape, and which cannot produce volatility that rises with the horizon (§3.9).

**Not a replica.** The model takes its primitives from the tape, but its strategic structure — sniper and maker latencies, the jitter, the number of racing firms and how makers quote — is designed and swept, because a public tape records neither failed orders nor who sent what (Aquilina, Budish, and O'Neill, 2022). The snipe-to-quote ratio shows the gap: the tape measures a race against aggregate displayed depth, the model against the quotes of the makers it contains, and how many makers rest at the touch no public feed reports. One asset on one venue is studied. Every verdict in §4 is a statement about this mechanism at this operating point, and carrying it to a venue is an assumption.

### 6.3 Future work

Three extensions follow from §6.2. Order flow with clustering and metaorders — a self-exciting arrival process and split parent orders — would test whether the verdicts depend on Poisson flow. A fee schedule for the fastest firms, rather than the public tier, would locate the model's rents against what those firms pay. And an engine whose book re-centres and whose uncross references the last print, with an option to skip the reference step, would let the batch arm run BCS's clearing rule natively rather than through a reprice (`paper/batch_interval1_note.md`). Beyond these, one asset on one venue leaves cross-venue latency arbitrage, where snipers compete across books, untouched.
